3-2-1 Backup in Practice — How Not to Lose Your Data at Home and at Work

28.09.20269 min read

Cloud sync is not a backup. Here's how to build a backup that survives a dead drive, a stolen laptop and ransomware — at home and in a small office.

There are two kinds of people: those who back up, and those who haven't lost their data yet. It sounds like a forum joke, but anyone who has ever recovered files after a dead drive or ransomware knows it's simply true.

This guide is for two groups: you at home (photos, documents, remote work) and the small company or office where "the computer person" is often just one person. The rules are the same; only the scale differs.

Sync is not a backup

The most common misunderstanding: "everything's on OneDrive / Google Drive, so I have a copy". Not quite. Sync copies every change — including the bad ones. Delete a folder by mistake? It disappears everywhere. Ransomware encrypts the files on your drive? The encrypted versions will dutifully upload to the cloud.

Cloud services have a recycle bin and version history, which often save the day — but they only work for a limited time and on the provider's terms. Treat them as an extra layer, not as your only copy.

⚠️ A simple test: if someone steals your laptop and your cloud account password leaks on the same day — do you still have your photos? If the answer is "I don't know", read on.

The 3-2-1 rule (and its newer version)

  • 3 copies of your data — the original and two backups,
  • 2 different media — e.g. the drive in your computer plus an external drive or NAS,
  • 1 copy off-site — in the cloud or on a drive kept somewhere else.

Because of ransomware, two more digits are added to this rule today: 3-2-1-1-0.

  • +1 offline or immutable copy — a disconnected drive, or a copy that can't be deleted or overwritten for a set period (so-called immutable backup). Ransomware can't encrypt what it can't reach.
  • 0 errors on restore — a backup is only worth something once you've checked that you can actually recover data from it.

Backup at home — a setup that's enough

1. A local copy on an external drive

  • Windows: File History (Control Panel → File History) automatically copies your user folders to an external drive and keeps previous versions.
  • macOS: Time Machine — plug in a drive, turn it on, forget about it.
  • Linux: Déjà Dup (GNOME) or, for the more technical, restic or BorgBackup.
💡 Important: disconnect the drive once the backup finishes. A permanently connected drive gets encrypted by ransomware along with everything else. Two drives in rotation (one at home, the other e.g. at your parents' or at work) are a cheap way to meet the whole 3-2-1 rule.

2. A cloud copy

  • Backblaze Personal Backup — $9/month or $99/year, unlimited data, runs in the background (Windows and Mac).
  • Proton Drive or iCloud — convenient, but primarily sync. Check how long they keep version history and deleted files.
  • For the technical: restic or rclone + cheap object storage (e.g. Backblaze B2) — full control and encryption on your side.

3. Don't forget the "invisible" things

  • 2FA backup codes — print them and put them away. Without them, losing your phone can mean losing your accounts.
  • Your password manager vault — Bitwarden and others let you make an encrypted export. Keep it offline.
  • Photos on your phone — check that they're really being backed up, not just "optimising storage".

Backup in a small business

In a company, it's not just files at stake but whether anyone can work tomorrow. The minimum I recommend for an office of a dozen to a few dozen people:

  • A NAS with snapshots — Synology, QNAP and similar devices can take read-only snapshots every hour or every day. If ransomware encrypts a network share, you roll back to a snapshot from before the attack.
  • An off-site copy — replication to a second NAS in another location, or to cloud storage with immutability turned on (e.g. Object Lock in S3-compatible storage).
  • Microsoft 365 / Google Workspace backup — the provider keeps the service running, but restoring a mailbox deleted six months ago is usually your job. Consider a separate tool to back up cloud mail and drives.
  • Configuration backups — firewall, switches, domain controller. I cover how to do it for FortiGate in the FortiGate admin guide, and for the domain in the Samba AD DC guide.
🚨 Keep backup separate from the domain: an attacker who takes over a domain admin account deletes the backups as one of the first things they do. The backup NAS shouldn't be accessed with the same account as the rest of the network. A separate account, a strong password, 2FA and — if possible — no access from the user network.

Restore testing — the step everyone skips

A backup you've never restored is just hope. Set a simple routine:

  • Once a month, restore one random file from each copy and check that it opens.
  • Once a year (in a business: once a quarter), do a full drill: restore a whole computer or server onto different hardware or a virtual machine. Measure how long it takes.
  • Write down the procedure — a sheet titled "how to restore the data", stored together with the backup password. In the stress after an attack, nobody remembers the details.
💡 Encrypt your off-site copies: a drive in a drawer at your parents' or a copy in the cloud should be encrypted (BitLocker To Go, FileVault, encryption in restic/Borg). Keep the password in your password manager and on paper — without it, an encrypted backup is useless to you too.

Checklist

  • I have a copy on an external drive and disconnect it after each backup
  • I have a copy outside my home/office (cloud or a second drive elsewhere)
  • At least one copy is offline or immutable
  • Off-site copies are encrypted and the password is written down in two places
  • My 2FA backup codes are printed
  • I've restored at least one file this month
