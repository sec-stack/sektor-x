Which VPN Actually Doesn't Log? I Checked the Audits and Court Cases of 7 Services

12.05.2026Updated: 27.09.20268 min read

Every VPN claims “no logs”. But a promise in the terms of service isn't proof. I collected what can actually be verified: independent audits and cases where police or a court actually came knocking.

The VPN market is flooded with marketing. “No-logs policy”, “military-grade encryption”, “your privacy is our priority” — every provider repeats the same slogans. A promise is one thing; practice is another.

To be upfront: this is not a lab test. I didn't measure speeds or hunt for DNS leaks. It's a review of publicly available evidence — audits, reports and court cases — with links to the sources at the end so you can check for yourself.

Criteria

  • External audit — has the no-logs policy been verified by an independent firm?
  • Real-world test — is there a known case where police or a court demanded data and got nothing?
  • Jurisdiction — where the company is registered. It matters, but less than you think: Mullvad is in Sweden (a “14 Eyes” country), and police still left empty-handed. What counts is whether the data exists at all.
  • Open source — is the app code open and verifiable?

My top 3

1. Mullvad — best for privacy

The industry benchmark. No email needed — you get a random account number. You can pay with cash in an envelope or Monero. In April 2023 Swedish police arrived with a warrant and left empty-handed, because there was nothing to take.

💡 Price: €5/month, one plan, no “-80% for 3 years” gimmicks. Mullvad regularly commissions audits — the latest public one (Assured Security Consultants, August 2025) covered its web app and found no critical, high or medium issues.

2. Proton VPN — best balance of price and privacy

Swiss jurisdiction (outside the EU and US), a free plan with no data cap, open-source apps. The no-logs policy is audited yearly by Securitum, a Polish security firm.

⚠️ Note: in 2021 Proton Mail handed over a French activist's IP address on a Swiss court order. Proton VPN falls under different rules than email and has no logging obligation — but the story is worth knowing.

3. IVPN — for advanced users

Less known but very solid. WireGuard and OpenVPN, no email to sign up, independent audits, open code. Downsides: fewer servers and a higher price than the big players.

⚠️ NordVPN — fine, with an asterisk: six independent no-logs assessments (latest: Deloitte, Nov–Dec 2025) confirm no logging. Its owner, Nord Security, grew out of the Tesonet group, which is also linked to Oxylabs (proxies and data scraping). There's no evidence of data sharing — it's just worth knowing. There have also been US class actions over auto-renewal practices.

What to watch out for

🚨 Kape Technologies: ExpressVPN, PIA and CyberGhost belong to one company — Kape Technologies (formerly Crossrider, whose tools were widely abused by adware developers). Kape also bought the publisher of popular “independent” VPN review sites. The services themselves have good evidence: the ExpressVPN server seized in Turkey in 2017, during the investigation into the Russian ambassador's assassination, contained no logs, and PIA twice had nothing to hand over in US cases. My issue isn't the tech — it's who gets your money and whose rankings you trust.

Free VPNs: if a service is free and has no paid tier funding it, you are the product. Most such apps collect and sell data. The exception is Proton VPN's free plan, funded by paid subscriptions.

A VPN is not a silver bullet

A VPN hides your IP from the sites you visit and encrypts traffic from your ISP. It does not protect you from:

  • cookie tracking and browser fingerprinting,
  • malware on your device,
  • phishing,
  • DNS leaks if the VPN is misconfigured.
