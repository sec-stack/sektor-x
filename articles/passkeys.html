Passkeys — Passwordless Sign-in That Can't Be Phished

28.09.20267 min read

A password can be stolen, guessed or phished. A passkey can't. Here's how passkeys work, where to keep them and how to turn them on for your most important accounts in 10 minutes.

Passwords have two problems that never go away: people reuse them, and they can be phished. A password manager solves the first one. Passkeys solve both at once — and more and more services let you use them.

What a passkey is

Instead of a password, your device creates a pair of cryptographic keys for a given site:

  • the private key stays with you — on your phone, computer or in your password manager — and is never sent anywhere,
  • the public key goes to the service. Even if the service gets hacked, nobody can sign in to your account with the public key.

To sign in, you unlock the key the same way you unlock your phone: with a fingerprint, your face or a PIN. Your fingerprint and face scan never leave the device — they are only used to unlock the key locally.

Why phishing stops working

This is the biggest advantage. A passkey is bound to a specific domain. A passkey created for google.com simply won't work on g00gle-login.com — the browser won't even offer it. There's nothing to "type in" on a fake page, so there's nothing to steal.

💡 For comparison: an SMS code or a code from a 2FA app can be phished — the scammer asks you for it on a fake page and immediately uses it on the real one. With a passkey, that trick doesn't work. That's why in the articles on spotting phishing and in the security checklist I recommend passkeys as the best option whenever a service offers them.

Microsoft reports that 98% of sign-in attempts with a passkey succeed, compared with only 32% with a password. Since May 2025, new Microsoft accounts are passwordless by default.

Where to keep your passkeys

  • Android and Chrome: Google Password Manager — passkeys sync across devices signed in to the same Google account.
  • iPhone, iPad, Mac: the Passwords app / iCloud Keychain — syncs across your Apple devices.
  • Windows: Windows Hello stores passkeys locally on the computer. Since the November 2025 update, Windows 11 also lets you save passkeys directly in 1Password and Bitwarden (Bitwarden's integration launched in beta).
  • A password manager (Bitwarden, 1Password, Proton Pass and others) — the best option if you mix systems, e.g. Android + Windows + iPad. One vault, every device.
  • A hardware key (e.g. YubiKey) — for your most important accounts and for anyone who wants the key physically on them.

Getting started — three accounts first

1. Google account

The fastest way: type g.co/passkeys into your browser — it takes you straight to the passkey settings of your Google account (Security → the section on how you sign in) → Create a passkey. On an Android phone signed in to that account, a passkey is often already set up.

2. Microsoft account

Go to account.microsoft.com → Security → sign-in options and add a passkey. Once everything works, you can remove the password from the account entirely.

3. The account you use most

Look for "passkey" in its security settings — Apple, Amazon, GitHub, PayPal and many online shops already support them. A list of services is kept at e.g. passkeys.directory.

What to watch out for

  • Losing your phone. If your passkeys live on a single device without sync, you can lose access. Keep them in a synced manager or add two passkeys (e.g. phone + hardware key).
  • Recovery methods. Keep a working recovery email or phone number and save your backup codes. These become the weakest link — protect them.
  • It doesn't work everywhere yet. Some services (including many banks) still require a password or their own app. There, keep using a password manager and 2FA as before.
  • "Add a passkey" in a suspicious email is still phishing. Only add passkeys from the settings of an account you opened yourself.

Checklist

  • I know where I'll keep my passkeys (password manager or Google/Apple account)
  • I've added a passkey to my Google or Microsoft account
  • I have a second way to sign in in case I lose my phone
  • My backup codes are printed and stored safely
  • I've checked whether my email and my most-used shops support passkeys
