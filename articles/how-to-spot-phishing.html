How to Spot a Phishing Email Before You Click — 7 Red Flags

07.05.20255 min read

Phishing is still the most popular attack method. Learn to recognize it in 5 minutes — and never fall for a fake PayPal or bank email again.

In 2024, over 90% of cyberattacks on businesses and individuals started with phishing. Not an advanced exploit, not a virus — just a plain email. The good news: phishing has characteristic signs you can learn to spot.

Red Flag 1: Sender address doesn't match the name

This is the most important signal. The displayed name can be anything — what matters is the actual email address. Click on the sender's name to reveal it.

🚨 Example: The display name says "PayPal Security" but the address is security@paypal-alert.ru — that's phishing. The real PayPal only sends from @paypal.com.

Red Flag 2: Urgency and fear as tools

Phishing operates on emotions. Fake emails are designed to cause panic and rush — so you click before you think.

  • "Your account will be suspended in 24 hours"
  • "Unauthorized transaction detected — act now"
  • "Your package will be returned — pay the surcharge"
  • "Final warning before account deletion"

Legitimate organizations don't operate on fear and don't demand immediate action via email.

Red Flag 3: Suspicious link — hover before you click

Before clicking any link in an email — hover your mouse over it and check where it actually leads (it'll appear in the bottom left of your browser or in a tooltip).

⚠️ What to look for: The domain must be exactly right. paypal.com is the correct address. paypal-secure.com, paypal.com.verify.ru, pay-pal.com — all phishing.

Red Flag 4: Language errors and strange characters

Many phishing attacks originate from overseas. Watch for:

  • Awkward phrasing that sounds like machine translation
  • Grammar and punctuation errors
  • Inconsistent capitalization
  • Strange symbols or encoding artifacts

Red Flag 5: Request for personal data or password

No legitimate organization will ever ask for your password via email. Not your bank, not PayPal, not Google, not Apple. If an email asks for your password, card number, or SSN — it's phishing.

Red Flag 6: An attachment you weren't expecting

An unsolicited attachment is a red flag. Especially dangerous formats:

  • .exe, .bat, .cmd — executable files
  • .doc, .docx with macros enabled
  • .zip containing any of the above
  • PDFs asking you to "enable content"

Red Flag 7: Email isn't addressed to you personally

Real emails from institutions where you have an account usually include your name or partial account number. A generic "Dear Customer" or "Dear User" signals a mass phishing campaign.

What to do when in doubt?

  1. Don't click any links in the email
  2. Open your browser and go directly to the institution's website
  3. Call the official customer service number
  4. Report the suspicious email to your national CERT
